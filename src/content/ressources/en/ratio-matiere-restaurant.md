## The formula, line by line

The calculation takes two lines:

1. Consumption for the month = stock on the 1st + purchases excluding VAT for the month − stock on the last day of the month.
2. Food cost ratio = consumption ÷ sales excluding VAT for the month × 100.

The French trade press recommends one more step: take the food cost of staff meals and comped items out of consumption. Those meals bring in no sales and vary from month to month, so leaving them in distorts the comparison.

A few rules for a reliable figure:

- count stock on a fixed date, the same day each month, after the last service;
- value it at the purchase price excluding VAT on your latest invoices;
- use purchases excluding VAT, net of credit notes;
- keep kitchen and drinks apart, with two ratios: drinks have their own benchmarks, and a combined ratio can hide drift in the kitchen.

## Worked example: one month in a bistro

The figures are an example; use your own.

| Line                                              | Amount excl. VAT |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Food stock on 1 October                           | €4,200           |
| + Food purchases in October (net of credit notes) | €11,800          |
| − Food stock on 31 October                        | €5,100           |
| = Consumption                                     | €10,900          |
| − Staff meals and comped items (at food cost)     | €400             |
| = Consumption used                                | €10,500          |
| Food sales excl. VAT in October                   | €38,500          |
| **Food cost ratio**                               | **27.3%**        |

Using purchases alone, you would have found 11,800 ÷ 38,500 = 30.6%. Most of the difference comes from a large order delivered on 30 October and still in storage on the 31st (€900 more stock), the rest from staff meals and comped items (€400). The following month the effect reverses: without a stock count, November would look better than it is.

## Benchmarks, and how to read them

| Published benchmark                                                                                      | Range                                             | Source                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Purchases consumed, food and drinks, French traditional restaurants (2025 accounts of 28,784 businesses) | around 31% (average gross margin: 69.3% of sales) | French Order of Chartered Accountants, Baromètre Image PME                     |
| Restaurant food cost ratio, general case                                                                 | 25% to 35%                                        | French trade press (L'Hôtellerie Restauration) and an accounting firm (Keobiz) |
| Fine dining, premium produce                                                                             | up to about 40%                                   | Keobiz                                                                         |
| Pizzeria, crêperie                                                                                       | 20% to 25%                                        | Keobiz                                                                         |
| Drinks (beverage cost)                                                                                   | 20% to 30%                                        | Keobiz                                                                         |
| Food and drinks combined                                                                                 | ideally under 35%                                 | Keobiz                                                                         |

Apart from the first row, taken from company accounts, these are trade benchmarks, not official statistics. The trade press says so itself: outside chains, each restaurant has to find its own norm. A chain compares each restaurant with the others in the brand. An independent has no such comparison: their reference is their own past months. A 33% ratio can be healthy in a small room with low rent and too high elsewhere.

The right way to use a benchmark is to watch the trend. Calculate your ratio every month, on the same day, and look at how it moves over six months. A two-point rise with no menu change needs an explanation.

## Actual and theoretical ratio: where losses hide

If your [costing sheets](/en/resources/cout-de-revient-plat) are up to date, you can calculate a theoretical ratio: for each dish, the sheet cost times the number of portions sold, divided by sales. That is what you would have used if everything went to plan.

The gap between the two measures what gets lost along the way. Back to the bistro: according to its costing sheets and its October sales, it should have used €9,400 of food, a theoretical ratio of 24.4%. Its actual ratio is 27.3%. The 2.9-point gap is €1,100 of food a month that does not end up on plates you sold.

The causes tend to be the same: portions bigger than the sheet, products thrown away past their date, delivery errors never claimed, invoiced prices above the agreed price, costing sheets that are out of date.

## How to bring the ratio down

Start with what does not affect the guest:

1. [Check every delivery and every invoice](/en/resources/controle-factures-fournisseurs-restaurant). A missing quantity you never claim costs you twice: you pay for it, then you buy the product again.
2. Weigh portions. A scale at the pass for your best sellers, and the costing sheet on display.
3. Store by date (first in, first out) and log what gets thrown away for two weeks. You will know which products to order less often.
4. Shorten the menu if some products only serve one dish that hardly sells.
5. Review the price of dishes whose cost has gone up. Recalculate the sheet before changing the price.
6. Renegotiate or compare suppliers on the ten products that weigh most in your purchases.

You do not steer the ratio in one month. Change one thing at a time and measure the effect the following month.
