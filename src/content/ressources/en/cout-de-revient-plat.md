## Plate cost, food cost: what are we talking about?

In restaurants, "the cost of a dish" usually means the food cost of one portion: what you paid for the ingredients that end up on the plate. Wages, rent and energy are not split dish by dish. They are covered by the margin each dish brings in.

This figure is worked out on a recipe costing sheet (the French "fiche technique"). One sheet per dish, listing the ingredients, their weight per portion, their purchase price and the method. It is also a kitchen tool: everyone plates the same portion.

## Step 1. List the ingredients at their real weight

Write down each ingredient with the weight that actually goes on the plate, for one portion. Weigh it once during service, not from memory. Do not forget the sides, the sauce, and the bread if it is counted with the dish.

For small ingredients (oil, salt, pepper, herbs), you have two options: weigh them once on a ten-portion batch and divide, or add a small flat "seasoning" amount per portion. Pick one rule and apply it to every sheet.

## Step 2. Use the right purchase price

The right price is [the price excluding VAT on your latest invoice](/en/resources/controle-factures-fournisseurs-restaurant), net of discounts. If your restaurant is liable for VAT, which is the usual case, the VAT you pay on purchases is recoverable, so it is not part of the cost.

Then convert that price to a usable kilo. A raw product loses weight: trimming meat, peeling vegetables, boning fish. That is the yield. Weigh before and after preparation over two or three deliveries, then take the average.

The formula: usable price per kilo = purchase price per kilo ÷ yield. Beef bought at €16.50 per kilo excluding VAT with an 85% yield really costs €16.50 ÷ 0.85 = €19.41 per kilo served.

## Step 3. Add it up: the steak tartare example

Here is a complete costing sheet. The prices are an example; replace them with those on your invoices.

| Ingredient (1 portion)                 | Net weight  | Purchase price excl. VAT | Yield | Usable price per kg | Portion cost |
| -------------------------------------- | ----------- | ------------------------ | ----- | ------------------- | ------------ |
| Beef (rump)                            | 160 g       | €16.50/kg                | 85%   | €19.41              | €3.11        |
| Egg yolk                               | 1           | €0.25 each               | 100%  | n/a                 | €0.25        |
| Shallot                                | 20 g        | €3.00/kg                 | 90%   | €3.33               | €0.07        |
| Capers and gherkins                    | 20 g        | €8.00/kg                 | 100%  | €8.00               | €0.16        |
| Frozen fries                           | 200 g       | €2.20/kg                 | 100%  | €2.20               | €0.44        |
| Salad                                  | 40 g        | €6.00/kg                 | 100%  | €6.00               | €0.24        |
| Seasoning (mustard, oil, salt, pepper) | flat amount |                          |       |                     | €0.20        |
| **Cost per portion**                   |             |                          |       |                     | **€4.47**    |

## From cost to menu price: the multiplier

The multiplier is the inverse of the [food cost ratio](/en/resources/ratio-matiere-restaurant) you are aiming for. If you want ingredients to account for 25% of the price excluding VAT, the multiplier is 4. At 20%, it is 5.

| Target food cost ratio | Multiplier on cost excl. VAT |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| 20%                    | 5                            |
| 25%                    | 4                            |
| 28%                    | 3.57                         |
| 30%                    | 3.33                         |
| 33.3% (one third)      | 3                            |

To place your target: in the 2025 accounts of 28,784 French traditional restaurants analysed by the French Order of Chartered Accountants, gross margin averages 69.3% of sales and staff costs 37.3%. Purchases consumed, food and drinks together, therefore weigh around 31%. The French trade press gives a 25% to 35% benchmark for food. It is a rough guide: your rent and payroll set your own target.

For the tartare, with a 28% target:

1. Price excl. VAT = €4.47 × 3.57 = €15.96.
2. Price incl. VAT = €15.96 × 1.10 = €17.56 (10% VAT for a dish served on site).
3. Rounded on the menu: €17.50 incl. VAT, i.e. €15.91 excl. VAT.
4. Actual ratio: 4.47 ÷ 15.91 = 28.1%. Gross margin: 15.91 − 4.47 = €11.44 excl. VAT per plate.

Watch out for set menus that include an alcoholic drink: alcohol is taxed at 20% in France, so the menu price has to be split between the two rates. If the menu items are also sold à la carte, the tax authority accepts a split in proportion to the à la carte prices.

## Check the price against the market

The multiplier gives a starting price, not the final price. Compare with menus in your area and with what your guests are willing to pay for that dish. If the calculated price is too high, work on the recipe (portion size, side, a cheaper cut) rather than on that one dish's multiplier. If a best-seller brings in a thin margin, make up for it on another dish.

## Update the sheet when a price moves

A costing sheet ages fast. Back to the example: if beef goes from €16.50 to €18.50 per kilo excluding VAT, the portion costs €3.48 instead of €3.11. The dish now costs €4.84. At €17.50 including VAT, the ratio rises from 28.1% to 30.4%, and the menu has not changed.

Three habits:

- check the cost of your ten best-selling dishes with every new supplier price list or price increase;
- weigh yields again when you change supplier or cut;
- date every sheet, so you know when it was last calculated.
